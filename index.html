What is AI actually doing to learning?

In January 2026 we spent a month inside six international schools in Switzerland to find out, and wrote up what we found as an open report for educators.

Who we are

We are Niklas Obermüller and Hiroshi Tokunaga, second-year students in the Williams College Global Scholars Programme. We study computer science, though our interest is less in the technology itself than in what it does to the people who use it.

In January 2026 we surveyed 276 students and 149 teachers and interviewed administrators, teachers, and students across six international schools in Switzerland, and published the findings as an open report. Hiroshi graduated from one of those schools, Aiglon College, in 2024.

We founded the AI Club at Williams, now more than 130 members, and have presented this research to the College's president.

What we did

A mixed-methods study across Le Rosey, Aiglon, La Garenne, Le Régent, the International School of Lausanne, and St. Charles. We surveyed 276 students and 149 teachers at five of the schools, then spent time on site at all six, interviewing administrators, teachers, and students.

94%

of students use AI during the school week. For most it is routine, not experimentation.

The heaviest users are the most uneasy.

The students who lean on AI most are also the ones most worried about what it is doing to their own thinking.

Teachers expect their role to change, not to disappear.

Few fear being replaced. What most anticipate is a fundamental reshaping of what teaching is for, and that prospect, more than any threat to their jobs, is where their unease sits.

What we concluded

Teachers matter more than policy.

The schools most in command of AI were not those with the strictest rules, but those whose teachers were confident using it.

Guiding beats banning.

Banning is neither workable nor desirable. The better response is to teach good use and rebuild students' confidence in their own thinking.

Assessment has to move to the process.

Grading a finished essay or problem set no longer shows what a student can do, because the finished product can be generated. The schools furthest ahead are shifting to oral and in-class work that makes the thinking visible.
